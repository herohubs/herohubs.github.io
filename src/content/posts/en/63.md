When a software product starts losing ground, with customers renewing less often, a backlog growing faster than the team can work through it and margins thinning quarter after quarter, the instinctive reaction is almost always the same, which is to cut. Hiring gets frozen, the module nobody seems to use gets shut down, the migration that has been waiting for two years gets postponed again, and everyone hopes that lower costs will be enough to bring the numbers back into balance. The 80/20 CEO by Bill Canady, a manager who built his career leading turnarounds in the industrial sector, starts from exactly this reaction in order to take it apart, arguing that pruning without a strategy only leads to chaos. The book talks about plants and sales networks, but its method translates surprisingly well to the world of software, where the temptation to cut blindly is just as strong and the damage is often harder to see.

##### Observe before you cut

Canady suggests starting with what he calls the Three L Tour, a walk through the company built around three verbs, listen, learn and leverage. Before deciding anything, the new leader meets people, reads the reports, compares the results with the company's history and only then acts on the truly urgent problems, moving resources from peripheral projects to central ones. In an organisation that builds software this tour has a very concrete equivalent, which runs through conversations with the support staff and the people writing the code, but also through telemetry, usage logs and the tickets of the last twelve months. Crossing these sources is often enough to discover that the feature management considers strategic is opened by a handful of users, while a CSV export added almost by accident single handedly supports the workflow of the most important customers. One of the most useful observations in the book concerns censorship and bureaucracy, which become enemies in a crisis, because problems must be able to travel upwards directly without being softened at every level of the hierarchy.

##### The product quadrants

The core of the method is segmentation. You collect last year's sales data, rank products and customers by volume, split each list into four equal parts and look at how revenue and margins are distributed. According to Canady the first quarter usually accounts for 85 to 90 percent of revenue, while the other three share what is left. Crossing customers and products produces four quadrants, and the one that really matters is the first, where the best customers buy the most profitable products. That is where eighty percent of the company's capacity should go, while the intermediate quadrants receive just enough effort to keep generating revenue, through higher prices, automated processes and people moved towards the first quadrant.

Applied to a software house or a SaaS product, the exercise is illuminating and also a little painful. Products become modules and features, revenue is read through subscription plans, and costs include not only infrastructure but also support hours, code to maintain and the customisations promised during the sales process. The least profitable quadrant almost always contains customers who pay little, ask for a lot and keep alive branches of code that nobody else uses, and every hour spent on those branches is an hour taken away from the customers who keep the company going. Losing a few customers feels counterintuitive, but sometimes it is the only way to free the resources needed to serve the ones that matter. In software this choice takes the form of an announced deprecation, a guided migration to a different plan or a redesigned price list, and technical debt stops being a purely engineering concern to become a portfolio decision.

##### A hundred days in phases

The second half of the book turns the analysis into a hundred day plan, an expression Canady borrows from Franklin Delano Roosevelt, who in 1933 pushed a wave of measures against the Great Depression through Congress in a few months, and which in turn came from Napoleon's Hundred Days of 1815, which however ended at Waterloo, a detail that reminds us that speed alone guarantees nothing. The plan goes through four steps, namely goal, strategy, structure and action. Ten days are spent setting a measurable target, because success cannot remain a feeling, and thirty building a strategy that, for a company in trouble, almost always means simplification. Structure comes only afterwards, and the book insists on a point that anyone who designs systems knows well: the organisation must be shaped around the priority, meaning the A customers buying A products, otherwise resources get scattered. It is a managerial version of Conway's law, and it is worth reading it in reverse as well, because teams designed around marginal modules will end up making exactly those modules grow.

The last step is the action plan, built by answering three questions for every initiative: who drives it, what it takes to deliver it and when it must be completed. Anyone who has worked with a technical roadmap recognises the pattern, and also recognises the difference between a wish list and a plan in which every item has an owner, a budget and a deadline, along with the flexibility needed to correct course when the data contradicts the initial assumptions.

##### Consensus before the decision

There is a detail in the book that would deserve more space than the author gives it, nemawashi, the Japanese practice of gathering the views of everyone involved before making a decision, so that by the time it is announced the ground has already been prepared. Software development has a codified form of it in RFCs and Architecture Decision Records, documents that lay out the problem, the alternatives considered and the reasons behind the choice, and that circulate the proposal before it becomes a fait accompli. Applying the 80/20 method to a product almost always means telling someone that their module will be scaled back, and doing so through a written and transparent process reduces resistance far more than any motivational meeting.

##### A discipline, not a cut

The book's limit is the one typical of the genre, an optimism that treats every crisis as solvable in three months and leaves in the background the cases in which the problem is not the portfolio but the market itself. Its merit, however, is to shift attention from cost reduction to understanding where value is created, and for anyone leading a technical team that is a valuable reminder. Before deciding which service to switch off or which refactoring to postpone, it pays to know, with data in hand, which twenty percent of the system supports eighty percent of the business, because that is where every hour of development delivers its highest return.
