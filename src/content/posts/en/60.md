In many companies, in an office next to the warehouse, there is still a black screen with green characters where someone enters orders and checks stock at an impressive speed, using key combinations learned by heart years ago, while a few metres away the online store, the customer management system and the sales team's app live in a completely different world. That screen is the front door of the AS/400, the system IBM launched in 1988 which, after changing its name several times up to today's IBM i, has never left the stage. To anyone who arrived later it looks like a museum piece, but for the people running the company it is where decades of orders, inventory and invoicing live, and switching it off even for a single day is unthinkable. The real question, then, is not whether to throw it away but how to bring it into the present without stopping the work that runs on it.

##### Why it is still there

Writing the AS/400 off as a relic underestimates the reasons it has lasted so long. The database is part of the system itself, and on top of it decades of programs have piled up that describe how the company really works, with its price lists, discounts, shipping rules and the exceptions agreed over the years with long-standing customers. Holding it all together is a degree of backward compatibility that few systems can match, because IBM designed it from the start to keep programs separate from the hardware they run on, so much so that in 1995, when the company switched to an entirely different kind of processor, customer applications kept working without anyone having to rewrite them. Nor is it an abandoned platform, given that IBM keeps developing it and the latest version came out in the spring of 2025. The result, especially in manufacturing and distribution companies, is often a system that simply never stops, and that very reliability is what makes every change so delicate.

##### The machine is not the problem

What pushes companies to change is rarely the system itself and almost always everything around it. The people who know those programs are retiring, and they take with them rules that are often written down nowhere but in the code. The links to the outside world have grown by accretion, through files exchanged overnight and hand-built bridges nobody dares to touch, so every new initiative, whether an online store, a customer portal or an app for the sales force, needs yet another custom connection. Then there is the green screen, lightning fast for someone who has used it for twenty years and baffling for everyone else, which makes even training a new colleague hard. When a company wants to show live stock availability on its website, the problem is not that the AS/400 lacks the data but that there is no simple and safe way to ask for it.

##### Why rebuilding everything from scratch is the riskiest bet

The most natural temptation is to rebuild everything, with a new ERP or a custom application replacing the old system in one go, and it is almost always the riskiest path. The old programs contain rules nobody remembers asking for, such as the special treatment for the customer who pays at ninety days or the rounding the accountant insisted on twenty years ago, and each of them resurfaces as an error only when the new system ignores it, perhaps on an invoice that has already gone out. Meanwhile the company keeps working and changing, so whoever is rebuilding the system is chasing a target that moves every month. In the end everything rides on a single switchover, often squeezed into a weekend, where the risks accumulated over months of work all come due at once and going back becomes extremely hard.

##### Building around instead of tearing down

The alternative is a gradual path that leaves the AS/400 where it is and builds a way out around it. The first step is to place a kind of single front desk in front of it, an entry point to which the website, the portal and the apps send their requests without knowing who actually answers, and which at first simply passes them on to the old system using the tools IBM i already provides to talk to the rest of the world. From there the work proceeds the way the strangler fig does, the plant that grows around a tree until it takes its place and that gave this approach its name among practitioners: one function at a time the new replaces the old, the front desk starts sending those requests to the new system and the old program stays on for as long as it is needed, ready to take over again if something goes wrong. Next to the front desk works a translator, which converts the way the AS/400 represents information, with dates stored as plain numbers and order statuses encoded as a single letter, into the language of modern applications, so that what is built for the next twenty years does not inherit the limits of the last twenty.

##### One piece at a time, data included

For this to work, the new system has to describe what it needs in its own terms, for example knowing the status of an order, without caring who will provide the answer. Today the answer comes from the AS/400, tomorrow it will come from a new service, and moving from one to the other works like changing the supplier of a component, perhaps trying it first on a share of the orders, without the rest of the application noticing. Readers who want to dig into the technical side will recognise the principles of [hexagonal architecture](/en/posts/40) here. The same caution applies to data, and the first move is to let new services look up customers, items and stock levels without being able to change them, which carries no risk at all. Once a function truly moves to the new system, however, the two worlds have to stay aligned for as long as they coexist, and it helps that the AS/400 often keeps a log of every change, originally meant for recovery and auditing, from which the new system can be updated almost in real time. The rule that prevents the worst problems is easy to state and harder to follow: every piece of information, at every stage, must have exactly one system allowed to change it, while the other only receives the updates.

##### Where to start, and when to replace everything

The first steps are less spectacular than one might expect, but they are the ones that decide whether the project succeeds. It starts by writing down what the system does today and which information the new channels ask for most often, then picking a first function with visible value and limited risk, such as order status shown to customers on the portal, and carrying it all the way through while carefully measuring its effect on the system before moving on to the next one. A full replacement, on the other hand, is not taboo. It makes sense when the company has changed so much that the old programs describe a way of working that no longer exists, when nobody can safely change them anymore, or when keeping two worlds alive consistently costs more than it returns. Even then the gradual path is not wasted effort, because the front desk and the translator built along the way are exactly what makes the final switchover manageable.
