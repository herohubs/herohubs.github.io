Anyone who has tried using a generative model to produce marketing material knows the exact point where the magic runs out. The first image arrives within seconds and is genuinely impressive, the second arrives just as fast but looks like it belongs to a different company, and by the tenth attempt more time has gone into rewriting the prompt to explain what the brand colours are, which tone to use and why that font is wrong than would have been needed to open a design tool and do the whole thing by hand. The problem is not the quality of the generation, which by now is taken for granted, but the fact that every request starts from nothing and that the identity of a business has to be reconstructed in words every single time. Pomelli, the experiment published by Google Labs together with Google DeepMind at the end of October 2025, attacks precisely this point, and it does so by shifting attention from the individual asset being generated to the profile that generates it.

##### Business DNA as the starting point

The first step is not writing a request but pointing the tool at your own website. Pomelli analyses it along with the images it finds there and derives what it calls the Business DNA, a profile that gathers the tone of voice, the colour palette, the fonts, the visual style and the way the business talks about itself and its products. It is the exercise any agency goes through in the first weeks with a new client, compressed into an automatic step that returns an editable document instead of a presentation. The profile is not a rigid constraint, it can be corrected where it misunderstood and enriched where it stayed generic, and from that moment it becomes the implicit context of everything produced afterwards.

The change of perspective is more interesting than it sounds. In a traditional workflow with a generative model the brand identity lives in the head of whoever writes the prompt and is transferred with effort, partially and differently every time. Here it is extracted once, made explicit, saved and reused, which means consistency stops depending on the memory and the patience of the person operating the tool. It is the same principle behind extracting configuration out of code in a software project, and it is worth noting because it is an architectural choice before being a product one.

##### From campaign ideas to assets

Once the profile is built, Pomelli proposes campaign ideas calibrated on the business, and this covers a problem that anyone running a small company knows well, namely having neither the time nor the headspace to decide what to talk about this week. You can pick one of the suggested directions or ignore them all and describe the goal in your own words, a launch, a seasonal promotion, the opening of a new location. From there the tool generates a series of materials ready for the channels, images with text designed for social posts, for the website and for ads, complete with captions and hashtags suited to platforms such as Instagram, LinkedIn and Facebook.

Every generated asset opens in an editor, and this is where it becomes clear that the ambition was never to build an image generator. You can replace the image by asking for a new one or by picking from those already collected in the Business DNA, rewrite the headline, the description and the call to action, adjust typography and the other visual elements, and rely on a command that fixes the layout when things end up out of place. Being a Google Labs product, Gemini stays available as a companion along the whole path, ready to suggest a variant or an opinion without leaving the context you are working in.

##### Photoshoot and product photography

February 2026 brought the addition that generated the most discussion. Photoshoot takes a single product photo, even an ordinary shot taken with a phone on the shop counter, and rebuilds it as if it had been produced in a studio. The mechanism combines the Business DNA with the Nano Banana 2 image generation model and offers a set of ready formats, from the studio shot to the floating object, from the breakdown of ingredients to a real scene of use. For a business selling physical products this is the point where the distance between the material available and the material desirable shrinks in a tangible way, because the bottleneck was never the layout but the fact that decent photographs cost time and money.

##### The limits that remain

It is worth being precise about what Pomelli is not. The quality of the output does not stand comparison with that of a capable designer who knows the brand, and anyone expecting to replace creative direction will be disappointed. Fine control over motion and animation is missing, which for certain uses is a real gap. The tool remains an experiment in public beta, free, available in English and limited for now to the United States, Canada, Australia and New Zealand, so from Europe it can be watched but not yet seriously adopted. The right measure for judging it is not the comparison with the work of a studio, but the ratio between time spent and the first usable piece of material, and that is where the gap with what existed before really shows.

Beyond the specific product, which being an experiment might evolve into something different or never reach us at all, the idea that stays is the persistent profile. For years we have treated generative models as interlocutors without memory, to whom everything has to be explained again every time, and most of the frustration comes from there. Extracting the identity of a company into a readable and reusable structure is a simple way of solving that problem, and the fact that it works for a small local business suggests it would work just as well for a software product, for technical documentation or for any context where consistency matters more than the single brilliant output.
