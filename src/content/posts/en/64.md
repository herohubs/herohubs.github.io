Every development team runs two evaluations of its people at the same time. The first is the official one, made of objectives, annual reviews and, in the best cases, a few agreed metrics. The second takes shape every day without anyone writing it down, during coffee breaks, in private messages and in comments made when the person concerned is offline, and it almost always arrives before the first. When a technical lead has to decide who gets the new project or who to put forward for a promotion, they already have an idea of everyone, and part of that idea comes from what they have heard rather than from what they have seen. For an organisation that builds software this parallel evaluation is a less obvious problem than it seems, because technical work is often hard to see, but it is also easier to correct, because that work leaves traces other professions do not.

##### What the research says

Organisational psychology treats office gossip as a genuine channel of social information, and in recent years it has started to measure its effects. A study by Cheng and colleagues, published in 2024 in the International Journal of Contemporary Hospitality Management, followed 379 hotel workers in Guangzhou across several waves and found that negative gossip slows career growth precisely by damaging reputation. Hauke and Abele-Brehm showed in three experiments in 2019 that not all gossip weighs the same, because gossip about a person's morality, meaning their fairness and trustworthiness, is the kind that most worsens the listener's impression of them. The 2022 meta-analysis by Wax, Rodriguez and Asencio, which pools 52 studies and more than fourteen thousand people, confirms that negative gossip is associated with worse outcomes than positive gossip, above all for the people being talked about. Finally there is a boomerang effect, documented in 2024 by Zhang, Ibrahim and Basu in five experiments with 1,400 participants: people known for speaking ill of others are consulted less as experts, even when their competence is obvious.

##### Why it weighs more in technical teams

Software development has a trait that amplifies the problem, namely a large amount of valuable and almost invisible work. Someone who spends a week stabilising a test suite, carefully reviewing colleagues' pull requests or fixing a service nobody wants to touch produces real value but rarely talks about it in meetings, while someone who gives a good demo or is always present in discussions builds a faster reputation that does not always match what they delivered. In this information gap the informal story takes over, and a passing remark such as "that module is theirs and it is a mess" can outweigh months of solid work. Remote work has not removed the coffee machine, it has moved it into direct messages, where conversations are even less visible and therefore even harder to balance.

##### Code leaves traces

What sets this profession apart is that almost everything a developer does leaves a verifiable trace. A pull request shows what changed and why, a review shows the quality of the feedback given to colleagues, a postmortem tells who diagnosed an incident and how, and an Architecture Decision Record keeps the alternatives considered and the reasons behind a choice together with the name of whoever proposed it. These traces are not meant to measure people by lines of code or number of commits, a practice that only produces distorted behaviour, but to give whoever evaluates concrete material to set beside their impressions. When someone claims a module is a mess, the repository history shows who actually wrote it, under which conditions and with which constraints, and it often tells a very different story.

##### Making invisible work legible

The next step is to design the process so that this material exists and is easy to find, with the same logic by which a pipeline enforces a rule instead of leaving it to memory. Code reviews held in public, on the platform rather than in a private chat, make visible who helps others improve. Blameless postmortems, which Google's Site Reliability Engineering book turned into a standard, shift attention from people to systems and take away the material for stories about who got it wrong. Technical decisions discussed in open channels and recorded in ADRs prevent a choice from being claimed or disowned after the fact, while a personal record of achievements, kept up to date by each person during the year, brings facts instead of memories to the performance review. None of these practices is new, but they are rarely presented as a defence of people's reputation, and that is precisely the job they do best.

##### What is left for people to do

No process will eliminate gossip, which also has a legitimate function, because it helps people understand how an organisation really works and who decides what. The realistic goal is a different one, namely making sure that whenever a decision about a person is taken there is always evidence stronger than the story, and that whoever leads the team has the habit of asking to see the pull request before trusting a sentence heard in the corridor. For a technical lead the question is simple: if they had to defend a promotion tomorrow in front of someone who does not know the person, would they have facts in hand or only impressions. A team that can answer with facts has already made everything said when someone is not in the room far less dangerous.
