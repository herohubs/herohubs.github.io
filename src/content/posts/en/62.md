Anyone who switched on a PC in the mid nineties remembers six seconds that need no introduction, a chord that rises and slowly opens up while the Windows 95 logo appears on screen, and that for millions of people was the first sound they ever associated with a home computer. It is less well known that it was composed by Brian Eno, one of the fathers of ambient music, and hardly anyone knows that he wrote it on a Mac. In the same years, on the other side of the fence, an Apple sound designer slipped a chord into the Macintosh ROM without asking anyone for permission, and that chord would go on to greet Mac users at every boot for a quarter of a century. Side by side, these two stories say a lot about how the details that end up defining a product's identity come to life, and about the surprising role that constraints play in creativity.

##### Three and a quarter seconds

In 1994 Microsoft was preparing Windows 95, and for the startup sound designers Mark Malamud and Erik Gavriluk decided to approach Eno, known for records such as Music for Airports and for his work as a producer alongside David Bowie, Talking Heads and U2. The brief he received through the agency, as Eno himself told the San Francisco Chronicle in 1996, was a long row of adjectives, because the piece had to be inspiring, universal, optimistic, futuristic, sentimental and emotional, and it closed with a far more concrete requirement: it had to last three and a quarter seconds. The request arrived at a time when Eno, by his own admission, was completely bereft of ideas, and for that very reason he appreciated someone putting a precise problem in front of him instead of yet another blank page. The constraint, which on paper looked like a cage, worked as a lever, and Eno became so absorbed in working on fractions of a second that he composed 84 different versions, refining timbres, attacks and tails with the attention usually reserved for an entire album. The version that was chosen lasts about six seconds, almost twice what was asked, and Microsoft adopted it anyway, so much so that thirty years later, in 2025, the Library of Congress added it to the National Recording Registry, the archive of recordings considered part of America's cultural heritage, in the same class as the Minecraft soundtrack.

##### The twist

The part that makes the story memorable surfaced years later, when Eno, appearing on the BBC, admitted that the sound had not been made on a PC. He had written it on a Mac, and with his trademark frankness he added that he had never used a PC in his life and did not like them. More than a provocation it is a lesson about tools, because the most recognisable sound of the Windows world came out of the rival ecosystem without any user ever caring: what reaches the listener is the result, not the platform it was produced on. Anyone leading a technical team knows the endless debates about editors, languages and operating systems, and this story is a reminder that a good professional works best with the tools they know inside out, even when they are not the client's.

##### A chord smuggled into the ROM

On the Apple side the protagonist is less famous but just as stubborn. Jim Reekes, a sound designer at the company, could not stand the startup sound of the Macintosh models of the time, built on a tritone, the interval that musical tradition has considered the most unsettling for centuries, and which he associated with the sounds the computer made when it crashed. In his home studio he prepared a C major chord on a Korg Wavestation EX and an Oberheim Matrix-6, designed to reassure the user rather than alarm them, and since he had no authorisation to change the sound he persuaded the engineers in charge of the ROMs to put it in shortly before release, so the new chime debuted on the Quadra 700 in 1991 and nobody ever took it out. That sound was also a piece of technical information, because it came after the hardware checks and confirmed that the machine was ready, while a different sequence of notes, nicknamed the chimes of death, announced a failure before the screen even lit up. Reekes is also behind Sosumi, one of the system alert sounds, born during the lawsuit that Apple Corps, the Beatles' company, had brought against Apple Computer: he had been asked to avoid names that hinted at music, and he chose "so sue me", spelled so that it would go unnoticed. In 2016 Apple dropped the chime from the new MacBook Pro models, but users were so attached to it that in 2020, with macOS Big Sur, the sound came back, on by default and with a setting to turn it off.

##### Constraint as direction

The two stories also share an epilogue, because for Windows Vista Microsoft turned to Robert Fripp, the King Crimson guitarist who had signed some of the most experimental records of the seventies with Eno, and who recorded hours of material from which Steve Ball, the project lead and his former student, extracted the final few seconds. In every one of these cases the constraint, whether it was three and a quarter seconds, a ROM already headed for production or hours of music to be reduced to an instant, did not hold creativity back but gave it a direction, and the same is true of software projects, where a clear scope almost always produces better solutions than boundless freedom.

In this instalment of RetroTech, the HeroHubs series on the technologies that shaped computing, the protagonist is a detail we have heard thousands of times without paying attention to it. The startup sound earns its place because it shows that a product's identity also lives in a few seconds crafted with obsessive care, sometimes by someone using the competitor's tools and sometimes by someone who never asked for permission. Which startup sound is still stuck in your head, and which small detail of your own products would deserve the same care?
